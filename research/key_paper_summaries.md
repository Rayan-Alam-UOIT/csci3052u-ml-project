# Key Paper Summaries

## Dunn, M., Terrill, A., Reesink, G., Foley, R. A., & Levinson, S. C. (2005). Structural phylogenetics and the reconstruction of ancient language history.

The paper aims to address the time limitations of studying the history of language family trees. Because of linguistic change, lexical cognate comparisons -words related in meaning and form- can only be used for this task at a time depth of approximately 8000 ± 2000 years. The authors aim to see if linguistic structural features could provide greater time depth. The authors constructed a questionnaire-based database. The completed data matrix contained 125 binary features for 15 Papuan and 16 Austronesian languages spoken in an overlapping region. The binary linguistic features allowed for the authors to use cladistic algorithms (maximum parsimony or NeighbourNet) to determine the possible phylogenetic relationships. From the author’s testing on subgroups with some lexical similarity, they show that cladistically analyzed grammatical structure can retain signals consistent with a known phylogeny. They then used the same method on a language set with no lexical similarity. These results formed a consensus tree that showed a very geographically consistent pattern. In spite of the absence of any lexical similarities, the results produced major clades that represent archipelagos and nearest neighbours being sister clades. The authors suggest that this method reveals evidence of large-scale genealogical clustering of the Island Melanesian languages. 

## Jäger, G., & Wahle, J. (2021). Phylogenetic typology.

The paper proposes an implementation of Maslova’s program using algorithmic techniques from biology, specifically the phylogenetic comparative method. The method uses many language families and isolates simultaneously, and uses whole phylogenetic structures of language families including branch lengths. They aim to establish a statistical framework that estimates typological trait distributions and feature dependencies. The authors assume that the change of typological features follows a continuous time Markov process (CTMC) meaning a language is in one of many states, and when mutations occur, the language switches into another state according to a probabilistic distribution. The authors extracted binary characters from lexical data from the Automated Similarity Judgement Program (ASJP) and used them in Bayesian phylogenetic inference to test for diachronic feature evolutions. For each feature pair they fitted a model where all lineages follow the same CTMC and a model if each lineage has its own CTMC. They first performed prior predictive sampling, then fitted for all 28 feature pairs, then performed posterior predictive sampling to test the fit, and finally performed statistical model comparison. The results show that the modeling of typological feature distributions in terms of phylogenetic CTMCs has many advantages for typology and the universal model provides a better fit. The authors conclude that their approach discounts the impact of larger families, giving more weight to smaller ones and isolate languages. They also conclude that since phylogenetic models only capture vertical transmission, language contact and other horizontal effects are ignored. 

## Khan, A., Shipton, M., Anugraha, D., Duan, K., Hoang, P. H., Khiu, E., Doğruöz, A. S., & Lee, E.-S. A. (2025). URIEL+: Enhancing linguistic inclusion and usability in a typological and multilingual knowledge base.

The study introduces URIEL+, an enhanced version of the URIEL knowledgebase and lang2vec focusing on improvements to typological features and typological distance. URIEL+ expands typological feature coverage to 2898 languages and improves many features including allowing for dynamic distance calculations. URIEL+ integrates five additional databases providing data for 8017 languages, and being able to calculate typological distances for 4366 of them. The authors also mention that each of these databases were preprocessed separately by binarization, combining redundancies and incorporating Glottocode identifiers. After this, URIEL+ still has 87% of values missing, so the authors include SoftImpute low-rank matrix approximation and dynamic customizable distance calculations within lang2vec. URIEL+ was validated using feature coverage analysis and imputation quality testing. The authors compare URIEL and URIEL+ on three downstream tasks. The first of which being LangRank, with URIEL+ consistently performing better than URIEL across all subtasks. The second being in LinguAlchemy with URIEL showing a small increase in performance across all subtasks. Finally, URIEL+ demonstrates competitive performance in ProxyLM with larger improvements in Unseen and Random settings. URIEL+ shows competitive performance across all downstream tasks when compared to URIEL especially when language-specific features are important. The results of  the provided case study show that URIEL+ distance calculations may have improved alignment of typological distance metrics in NLP. The authors conclude that URIEL+ provides improved robustness, usability, reliability and performance in downstream NLP tasks when compared to URIEL. Limitations of URIEL+ include its inherent dependence on quality of source databases, as they skew the focus on featural distance toward grammar, potentially underrepresenting phonological features. 

## Lin, Y.-H., Chen, C.-Y., Lee, J., Li, Z., Zhang, Y., Xia, M., Rijhwani, S., He, J., Zhang, Z., Ma, X., Anastasopoulos, A., Littell, P., & Neubig, G. (2019). Choosing transfer languages for cross-lingual learning.

The paper examines the challenge of selecting optimal high-resource transfer languages for cross-lingual transfer to low-resource languages. The authors note that a common challenge in NLP is applying techniques to low-resource languages due to their lack of training data, and that cross-lingual transfer has shown that it is possible to mitigate this; however, finding the optimal transfer language for any task language is still an open challenge. The authors introduce a framework called LandRank for finding the best transfer languages for a given low-resource language and NLP task. The authors take six linguistic distance vectors from URIEL with properties like subword overlap. They use the Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT) and LambdaRank models as their training method. The authors test the performance of LangRank on four NLP tasks; machine translation, entity linking, POS tagging and dependency parsing. Leave-one-out cross validation is used in each NLP task. The results show that theLangRank outperforms strong baselines and provides fairly good performance when baselines give no useful recommendation. They conclude that learned ranking models using multiple dataset statistics and language attributes make better recommendations for transfer languages than models only using single language or dataset features. 

## Ng, Y. H., Hoang, P. H., & Lee, E.-S. A. (2025). Less is more: The effectiveness of compact typological language representations.

The authors aim to create an optimization pipeline for URIEL+ typological feature space by combining feature selection and imputation. They note that the scope of URIEL+ expanded but its utility is constrained by the feature space. Many of the 800 features overlap or are strongly correlated. Past models have treated all features as equally informative which can cause noise, and change the similarity measures with uninformative signals. The authors aim to use feature selection to address this by prioritizing the most statistically and linguistically significant features and utilizing imputation to fill in the remaining gaps. They look to see if this dimensionality reduction yields more meaningful language distances. The optimization pipeline is made up of three stages. The first being feature selection using four supervised and unsupervised methods. The second is missing value imputation. The last stage being the evaluation of each reduced feature set’s impact on the performance of downstream tasks, specifically of cross-lingual transfer prediction, language model performance prediction and language model linguistic regularization. The results show that for LangRank, the Laplacian Score and CFS methods beat the full feature set baseline with respective gains of +7.8% at size 300 and +6.0% at size 100. While Variance and PCA Loading strategies yielded less improvement, it still demonstrates that the language distances calculated from these methods allow for the selection of better transfer languages than the baseline. Though Variance and PCA Loading preserve less structural information. The result of the optimization pipeline also never degraded the performance of any task demonstrating its robustness. The authors conclude that the optimization pipeline improves the informativeness across high-resource and low-resource languages, and the newly produced vectors produce better typological comparability, cross-lingual transfer and language model performance prediction accuracy. The authors mention the limitations that different linguistic dimensions may require unique representational strategies, each feature selection method contains biases, that SoftImput could better capture linguistic relationships, and that their evaluation focused on a smaller set of higher resource languages.
